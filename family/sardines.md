The Sardine and Tabasco Ritual

My cousins always talked about the Dairy Queen trips. When we would get together years later, they’d reminisce about how Grandpa took them to get ice cream, a ritual that defined their memories of him. I never went. My grandparents and my father ran a small convenience store, a precursor to the modern gas station model, complete with a deli and a full ice cream cooler just steps away. I didn't need a trip to Dairy Queen; I had access to the candy and the soda whenever I wanted it, which meant the allure of those things was non-existent.

Instead, my memories were made on the steps of the station. Grandpa would grab a can of sardines and a bottle of Tabasco, and we would sit there together—just the two of us—eating on the concrete. I was only four years old, playing with an old-fashioned adding machine that felt like a high-stakes tool, long before the days of screens and digital interfaces. When I brought this memory up to my cousins, they would give me a funny look and say, "Grandpa must not have liked you very much," assuming a lack of ice cream meant a lack of love. They were wrong. They were looking for the spectacle; I was participating in his reality. In those quiet moments with the sardines and the heat of the Tabasco, I knew exactly where I stood. I was his partner on the steps, and to me, he loved me best.
